About NCA
Founded in 1937 at the US Naval Observatory by a group of professional and amateur astronomers, the National Capital Astronomers (NCA) is a volunteer-driven nonprofit aiming to ignite public interest in astronomy and space science. We're all about sharing the wonders of the universe with everyone!
Our Stellar Activities:
Monthly Star Talks:
 Join us every second Saturday at the University of Maryland Observatory for enlightening lectures followed by stargazing sessions (weather permitting).
Exploring the Sky:
 In collaboration with the National Park Service, we host free monthly telescope events at Rock Creek National Park. Catch a planetarium show before diving into the cosmos!
Sidewalk Astronomy:
 We pop up around DC with telescopes for both planned and spontaneous events, showcasing celestial wonders against the city's bright lights.
Science Fair Fun:
 We scout and reward top-notch astronomy projects in local science fairs.
School & Scout Outreach:
 We bring the stars to classrooms and scout groups with talks and telescope demonstrations.
Telescope Making Workshops:
 Since WWII, we've been running free workshops where you can craft your own Newtonian telescope or fine-tune a commercial one.
Deep-Sky Adventures:
 Our members often venture to darker sites for deep-sky explorations with other astronomy clubs.
Affiliations & Contributions:
 As part of the Washington Academy of Sciences and the Astronomical League, we actively combat light pollution with Dark Sky International and are part of the Night Sky Network.
Our Cosmic Perspective:
While we marvel at the discovery of thousands of exoplanets, we recognize Earth's unique hospitality. This drives our mission to protect our planet's resources and life. After all, as Captain Kirk (a.k.a. William Shatner) discovered, space is a harsh place!
Join us for a cosmic journey and be part of our mission to explore, understand, and protect our universe!

Models of the Universe (what we can and cannot know, what do we know, how and when did we know it?)
Dr. James B. Cole (Leidos)
Next Meeting Date: Saturday, 14 November 2026
7:30 pm
Abstract:
 How can we infer the shape and structure of the universe? How much confidence do we have in our deductions about the greater part of the Universe which we cannot access?
"The miracle of the appropriateness of the language of mathematics to the formulation of the laws of physics is a wonderful gift, which we neither understand nor deserve," thus remarked Eugene Wigner, one of the greatest physicist / mathematicians of the 20 th Century (contributions to fundamental science on a par with Einstein).
Models too can have almost mystical powers. Quarks were first considered a convenient fiction to explain the multiplicity of subatomic particles, but they turned out to be real. Except for the infinitesimal fraction of the Universe, which we can directly access, our understanding is through models based on observations and inference following the laws of logic and mathematics, constrained by the almost miraculous efficacy of Occam's razor.
We briefly review the historical stepping stones to our current understanding from the philosophy of Anaximander, on through the models of Ptolemy, Copernicus, and Kepler; and finally the Big Bang.
How can we infer the shape and structure of the universe? We delve into a few technical details: How do we measure the size of stars and their distances; what do we (think) we know about how stars evolve and the Big Bang?
Bio:
 I began my professional career at the University of Maryland (PhD, particle physics). I became interested in the structure of the Universe as a teaching assistant in the Astronomy Dept. We taught some of pioneering methodologies of Henrietta Leavitt in the classes. I was awarded a National Research Council post-doctoral fellowship at the NASA-Goddard Space Flight Center. A NASA balloon experiment that had detected antiprotons in the cosmic rays. Many exotic theories about their origin were extant: arrivals from an antimatter galaxy, decay of exotic particles, etc. My branch chief supported a more prosaic explanation: high energy (ordinary) protons interacting with ordinary matter in the upper atmosphere. We proved this via detailed simulations and modeling of the experimental instrumentation, which incorporated the known high energy proton flux, and antiproton production cross sections

Exploring the Sky
 is a joint program between the National Capital Astronomers and the National Park Service Rock Creek Park Nature Center and has been run since 1948 at this location, the field at the corner of Glover and Military Roads in the District. There is an adjacent parking lot. It is free and all are welcome who have an interest in observing the heavens. It's not an ideal dark sky location but we can still see solar system objects (even the occasional comet), open and globular clusters and maybe a fuzzy galaxy or two.
Questions? Call NCA at 202-262-4274 and leave a message.
