Makomdc (Learning & Immersion) 2026-27
Judaism has never stood still. Across the centuries, visionary thinkers, daring mystics, bold artists, and towering legal minds have challenged, expanded, and transformed Jewish life — reshaping how we understand our tradition, our community, and ourselves. This year at MakomDC, we will explore the ideas and the people behind them: the scholars, spiritual leaders, halachic decisors, mystics, wisdom teachers  and cultural pioneers whose innovations left an indelible mark on Jewish thought and practice. We will learn from innovative scholars and our own clergy about the figures and ideas that changed everything. Through their lives and legacies, we will ask the questions that animate Jewish life in every era — What does it mean to reimagine a tradition? How does law evolve while remaining anchored to the past? What does it take to change a people? And what can these transformative figures teach us about our own moment?
Prepare for our year of learning by diving into our rabbis recommended reading.

Each year, MakomDC's themed teaching series invites our clergy to explore the year's central questions in depth. 
Rabbi Yaakov Yosef of Polnoye on the early Hasidic Approach to Parshanut
Taught by Rabbi Lauren Holtzblatt
Shabbat afternoons at 1pm, Biran Beit Midrash: November 14th and December 19th
The preeminent student of the Baal Shem Tov, the Toldot Yaakov (Rabbi Yaakov Yosef’s parshanut) was the first published Hasidic commentary on the Torah.  We will learn about his life as a student of the Besht and his approach to reading and interpreting the Torah.  This study will help to uncover the innovations that followed with Hasidic parshanut and how they differ from the medieval commentators.
Rabbi Kalonymous Kalman Shapira, The Piaseczno Rebbe
Taught by Rabbi Lauren Holtzblatt
Shabbat afternoons at 1pm, Biran Beit Midrash: April 10 and 17
In this class we will learn about the life and works of the Piaseczno Rebbe.  Rabbi Kalonymous Kalman Shapira was both the Grand Rabbi of Piaseczno and became the Rabbi of the Warsaw Ghetto.  His works that were written and delivered in the Warsaw Ghetto were buried in milk cartons found post war in the ghetto and are included in the Ringelblum Archive.  We will study the Esh Kodesh, Divrei Torah that were delivered to the Jews of the Warsaw Ghetto during the Shoah. 
The Birth of a New Old Judaism
Taught by Rabbi Aaron Alexander
Shabbat Afternoons at 1pm,  Biran Beit Midrash: Dec 5th, Feb 27, April 3, May 22
In order to appreciate the varieties of  the Jewish religion today, it is critical to understand post Enlightenment and emancipation Germany, where many of them emerged.  On October 22, 1873, the Hildesheimer Rabbinical Seminary opened in Berlin, and quickly became a foundational & innovative building block of modern-orthodox Judaism. Dr. David Elleson (z”l) reflected on the seminary’s founding Rector, Rabbi Azriel Hildesheimer: “Hildesheimer displayed a rich mixture of tradition and innovation, testifying to the complexity of the evolution of contemporary Jewish religion and society from the traditional patterns of medieval central European Judaism. Indeed, it is this interplay between the old and the new, the tensions and the congruities, that indicates Hildesheimer’s capacity to respond to the realities of the contemporary world while simultaneously affirming a commitment to traditional Jewish values.” The Seminary would exist until closed by the Nazis in 1938. 
In this series, we’ll study historically consequential texts (primarily legal) by each of the Seminary’s four Rectors–Rabbi Azriel Hildesheimer, Rabbi David Tzvi Hoffman, Rabbi Avraham Eliyahu Kaplan, and Rabbi Yehiel Yaakov Weinberg.  
Innovation by Design: What Prayer and the Siddur Teach Us About Jewish Change
Taught by Rabbi Sarah Krinsky
Tuesdays at 7:30pm, February 2, 9, 16, Biran Beit Midrash | 
Register Here
Prayer is one of Judaism's oldest traditions—but it has never been static. Every generation has reimagined the language, structure, and purpose of prayer to reflect new realities while remaining rooted in timeless values.
In this series, we'll explore the siddur not simply as a prayer book, but as a blueprint for Jewish creativity and innovation. Together, we'll uncover how the siddur as we know today was composed, adapted, and reshaped over centuries; examine the emergence of modern Jewish liturgy; and ask what it means to create authentic prayer in our own time. Along the way, participants will have the opportunity to experiment with writing their own prayers, drawing on the same literary and spiritual models that have guided Jewish prayer for generations.

Join us as we spend 7 Shabbat mornings journeying through the heart of Shabbat morning davening. We will explore the words and their meaning, the kavanah (intention and focus) each prayer asks of us, the theology beneath the liturgy, and the choreography that accompanies it. Session by session, we'll move through the service in order, uncovering what each section is asking us to think, feel, and become.
This monthly series takes one section of the Shabbat morning service each time — from the first waking blessings to the closing bows of Aleinu — and asks three questions together: What is the text actually saying? What does the choreography do to help or hinder the prayer? And what meaning might I garner from prayer work? No prior knowledge of Hebrew or liturgy required.

Shabbat Speaker: Judge David Tatel 
Author of Vision: A Memoir of Blindness and Justice
Shabbat October 31st at 1 pm, Biran Beit Midrash
Judge David Tatel served on the United States Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit from 1994 to 2024, succeeding future Supreme Court Justice Ruth Bader Ginsburg. After graduating from the University of Michigan and the University of Chicago Law School, he served as the founding director of the Chicago Lawyers’ Committee for Civil Rights Under Law and then director of the National Lawyers Committee. He headed the Office for Civil Rights of the US Department of Health, Education, and Welfare during the Carter administration and then founded and led the education practice at Hogan Lovells, where he is now Senior Counsel. 
Judge Tatel co-chaired the National Academy of Sciences committee on Science, Technology and Law from 2016 to 2023. He also chaired the boards of The Spencer Foundation and The Carnegie Foundation for the Advancement of Teaching. Judge Tatel is a member of the American Philosophical Society and the American Academy of Arts and Sciences. Judge Tatel and his wife, Edie, live in Castleton, Virginia and Washington, D.C. They have four children, eight grandchildren, and one great-grandchild.

"Shtisel, Shababnikim, and the Exciting Possibilities of New Israeli Television" 
 | 
Register Here
For the first two decades of Israel's existence, there was no television to speak of—David Ben Gurion viewed the medium as hopelessly decadent. It took another two decades until a second, commercial, channel was allowed to broadcast in 1986, and then almost two more until anything of value reached the airwaves. During that time, American TV entered into an era of real aesthetic ambitions and accomplishment with the production values to match. Finally, Israeli television has its own answer to hits like the "Sopranos" and "Mad Men," and in many ways, they are more sophisticated, more textured, and more literary than their American peers. In this talk, we'll focus on two hits populated by Haredim which are in one case serious, in another silly, yet always in touch with the depths of Jewish tradition.
Dr. Deborah Barer  
November 21 at 1pm, Biran Beit Midrash
Patriotic Disappointment: A Jewish Approach to American Commitment
What does it mean to love a country that disappoints us? Drawing on classical sources from the Tanakh and rabbinic literature, this talk explores how the Sinai covenant models a form of love that can withstand deep disappointment. Rather than undermining our commitments, moments of disillusionment and loss can become opportunities to deepen and refine them. Through an exploration of what the covenant both offers and demands, we will consider what it can teach us about sustaining commitment to the American project in uncertain times.
Deborah Barer is Senior Faculty at the Shalom Hartman Institute of North America. Prior to joining the team at Hartman, she was Associate Professor of Religious Studies at Towson University, and she has also taught at Oberlin College and for the Pardes Institute of Jewish Studies (North America). She holds a PhD in Religious Studies from the University of Virginia.
Deborah is the author of Going Off Script: Improvisational Judgment in the Talmud (Oxford University Press, 2026), which explores how the Talmud develops the idea of acting lifnim mi-shurat ha-din (lit., “within the line of the law”) and its implications for how we think about decision-making, Jewish law, and ethics.

A Journey Through Shabbat: Text, Poetry, and Song
February 12 - February 13
Join Roy Matalon for 3 special piyyut (liturgical poems/songs) teachings at  Friday Night Shabbat Festival, in the Smith sanctuary Shabbat morning and for a Shabbat afternoon 1 pm shiur.
Through the unfolding of Shabbat, we will encounter texts, poetry, melodies, and spiritual traditions that have shaped Jewish experience across generations.
Drawing on rabbinic and mystical teachings, the rich tradition of piyut, and the diverse musical voices of the Jewish people, we will explore the many moods and meanings of Shabbat—from the anticipation and longing of Kabbalat Shabbat, through the joy and fullness of Shabbat morning, the quiet depth of the afternoon, and the bittersweet transition as Shabbat draws to a close and we move into a new week.
Our learning, reflection, and song will open new pathways into the beauty, depth, and transformative power of Shabbat.
Rabbi Roly Matalon is Rabbi Emeritus of Congregation B'nai Jeshurun in New York City, where he completed a forty-year spiritual leadership tenure in 2026 as Senior Rabbi and Rosh Kehillah. Born in Buenos Aires and ordained at the Jewish Theological Seminary in 1986, he helped revive BJ and transform it into a national model of vibrant, participatory Jewish prayer. Rabbi Matalon is now the Founding Director of the Institute for Prayer and Piyut, dedicated to bringing transformative, soulful prayer and global Jewish music to congregations, clergy, musicians, and educational institutions across denominations and movements.

Tovanot: Rejecting the Binary, Embracing Dual Narratives
Sundays 12:00-3:00 pm, May 2nd, 16th, 23rd and June 6th | 
Register Here
Today’s conversations about Israel-Palestine are charged, polarized, and emotionally exhausting. For many, the discourse feels overwhelming and destabilizing. Jews of all ages find themselves torn between liberal values and Jewish identity, forced into binary choices that don’t reflect the complexity of their beliefs.
Tovanot (Hebrew for insights/takeaways) is a groundbreaking educational experience that offers a powerful alternative: a space to think critically, feel deeply, and engage with history and conflict with empathy and rigor. Tovanot rejects zero-sum thinking and facilitates a rigorous and sober exploration of Zionist history and connection to the Jewish homeland alongside concern and empathy for Palestinian lives, narratives, and connection to the land.
Through side-by-side readings of Jewish and Palestinian texts, maps, and narratives, reflective exercises, and brave conversations, participants dive deep into the most complex questions and are encouraged to hold nuance, sit with discomfort, and imagine win-win possibilities for Israelis and Palestinians.
Tovanot is a 2026 Covenant Foundation Signature Grant Recipient.
Your Educator: Dr. Ruti Kadish is an Israeli-born educator and scholar who has spent her career at the intersection of Jewish identity, Israel education, and community building. Her academic expertise and lived experience in Israeli and American Jewish life inform Tovanot—developed to fill a gap she saw firsthand: the need for honest, values-driven Israel education that doesn’t ask people to choose between their Jewish identity and their conscience.

The Michael Stern Adult Torah Study Scholarship Fund
Click Here to Apply!
The Michael Stern Adult Torah Study Scholarship Fund is intended to assist members of Adas Israel who seek to advance their understanding of the Jewish faith, in particular through study of our foundational texts together with commentaries from antiquity to the present.
Applicants may seek support to engage in any level of learning - from introduction to Judaism classes to advanced text courses. There is no age restriction for applicants. The award limit under most circumstances is $500 per participant.
Examples of organizations that offer relevant courses of study include the Jewish Study Center, Hadar, Svara, the Brandeis Bardin Institute, the National Havurah Committee including its Summer Institute, the Haberman Institute for Jewish Studies, and Aleph Beta. 
The Fund is jointly administered by Rabbi Kerrith Solomon and Rabbi Sarah Krinsky who are happy to entertain any questions you may have. Applications should be sent to them. The program is ongoing. Applications for 2021 and 2022 will be received and considered on a rolling basis. The application form is below. 
Michael Stern z”l was one of Adas Israel’s own great sages. He engaged in Torah study for its own sake, advancing to the level of “master teacher,” a designation conferred in 2018 by our senior rabbis.
His family has created the Fund to honor Michael’s lifelong love of learning and teaching.
